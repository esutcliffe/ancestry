Somewhere in France June 30, 1918 Dearest Hazel, It is Sunday and very quiet around here today, and I will write you a few lines, for I am going to leave here tomorrow and I may not have opportunity to write you for several days. It is a beautiful morning here. The roses are in full bloom, and you never saw roses bloom the way they do here. Every garden is full of them and they grow along the roadside. And the climbing roses just cover the old fashioned houses. It is a sight one can never forget. I wish we could grow roses like these in America. The climate is so favorable for them over here. They don’t even require any care. I was in the hayfield again yesterday and we are all through now putting up hay. I got up early this morning and put out a big washing. It is not at all hard to keep clean here, for this country is blessed with good, pure water. The water just rolls out of the mountains in large streams. There is a stream outside my billet 10 feet deep, and the water is as clear as a crystal, and it is full of mountain trout and bass. I have tried to catch some of them, but I haven’t had any luck yet. Albert is about 25 miles from here. I came through the place a few days ago but I didn’t have time to stop and see him. I am not speaking much French yet and I don’t think I ever will be able to speak this language, and I don’t care if I don’t. I am much more interested in the conditions and the methods of the country than the language. And even if we don’t learn to speak French, we will bring back a valuable education with us. I am wondering how soon I will have another letter from you. I hope it won’t be very long. By the way, dear. You have read of American Soldiers marrying French girls and bringing them back to America with them after the war. Well, I want to tell you they are few and far between. They are the bravest men and the best fighters you ever saw. A friend of mine sent me a copy of the Star. I got it a few days ago, and it was just like meeting a long lost friend. This is the first one I have seen since we left home, and I have read it, advertising and all. Dearest, I expect you are anxious to know what life is like at the Front, but I haven’t been close enough to it to be able to talk about it. However, I can tell you it is not all glory. Life at the Front is very simple. A man faces death every day and don’t think anything about it. But in spite of the hardship and suffering men go through here, you could not find a man who would go back home if he had the opportunity. I would like to be as optimistic about our return as you are, but we are seeing the war from a different angle over here, and I am not sure that we shall return as soon as you think we will. There isn’t a thing we could wish for to make us more comfortable. I have wished several times that I had an old magazine to read. Reading matter is one thing that we haven’t beenable to get much of yet, and at times when we don’t have much work to do, time goes by very slow. I haven’t been able to get in touch with the Stars and Stripes yet, but just as soon as I can, I am going to have it sent to you. I have learned through American newspapers that the Mothers, Sweethearts, and Sisters of the Boys have been a good deal concerned by reports of wrong conduct in our Army over here, and I want to say a few words about this to comfort you or anyof your friends who may have Boys over here. It is just another clever German scheme to breed discontent. I have lived with the Boys in training camps at home and seen service with them on the border, and I want to tell you they are the same Boys that they were in America and the same motives, the same ideals, the same purposes rule their lives. Cousance, France November 23, 1918 My dearest Hazel, Tomorrow has been designated as the day for the Soldiers to write their Fathers, and as it is not my good luck to have a Father or Mother, I am going to do the next best thing by writing to the dearest and best girl in the world. And as the censor has modified the rules since the signing of the Armistice, I am now allowed to tell you things that would never passed while we were fighting. And I am going to give you a brief history of my trip over here and my stay in France, and perhaps I may tell things that I have written before, and if I do you will excuse me I am sure. I landed in Liverpool June 1st and left there the same day and went to Rumsey, a small town about 6 miles from Southampton, and while I was there I saw that wonderful abbey, and I visited the grave of Florence Nightingale. We stayed there 5 days and then marched to Southampton, where we got a boat across the English Channel. It was just a 10 hour ride in the darkest night I ever saw. We landed in Havre, France June 6 early in the morning and stayed there 2 days. We were very tired and worn out and I never did have an opportunity to see the town. From Havre we took a train and traveled 3 days and nights and I never did know where we were. I didn’t have a map with me. I do know that we came within a few miles of Paris. We passed Paris on the north. We landed in a town of about 20,000 population by the name of Epinal, and we rested there in a French garrison for 36 hours. And then marched to our final destination, a small town named Elayes, and there we were introduced to our first French billets and it was there I met litle Margurite Marishall, and there I think was the prettiest scenery I ever say and the finest climate. It was the foothills of the Swiss Alps. We went in to training there and stayed there until July 16 when we marched about 50 miles north up to the Front. I think it was about July 20th when we took over the Front in Alsace, not very far from the Swiss border. We were not far from Mulhouse, and on clear days we could see the snow capped Alps in Switzerland. And the climate was wonderful although it rained nearly every day. But we had good dugouts there and it was a mild war compared to what we went through later on. At times we saw some hard fighting, but it was mostly local raids. We left that country the latter part of August, and went through Luneville, Taul, and Nancy, and got in on the St. Mihiel Front the night the drive started, and we were held in reserve there during the drive. We were not called in to action there, but we were ready. Our Boys met no resistance there. We camped in the woods a litle way outside Nancy for about a week and then we started out for the Argonne Forest. The big drive started on the morning of Sept 26th. The barrage started at 2:30 a.m. and we got to the Front at 5 a.m. and we can never forget that morning or the 7 days following. The cannons stood almost wheel to wheel and there were tanks and airplanes by the hundreds. At 7 a.m. our boys started over and before noon our men had a field hospital in operation in a captured town, and that night we were 7 miles beyond the town. We continued to advance until the 4th day when we had to slow up and wait for the artillery to catch up with us. The Boys fought 24 hours a day, always going forward and we met the very best of the German Army too. Nearly all of the Prussian Guard. The first 2 days we took many prisoners, but after that there were only a few taken. It was a fight to the finish and I think the deeds of the American Soldiers will live forever in the pages of American History. Well, I think you know what happened from that time till now, for I have written you many letters since. I am now living in an old factory between St. Mihiel and Verdun. I went through St. Mihiel a few days ago and I don’t think there is a building that has not been hit. I haven’t been in Verdun yet, but I think I will have an opportunity to go there in a few days. I am very anxious to see that place. It seems strange to be able to write and tell you where I am at after we have been restricted so long. I haven’t seen Albert since I came back. I think he is up close to Verdun. We are having the finest weather here now. It doesn’t rain as it used to and I believe all the firing and explosions were responsible for all the rain we had. It is cold here, but it is clear and the sun seems to help me more than all the medicine and nursing I got in the hospital, and I am feeling better and I think by the time I get back home I will be as well as ever. You asked me in one of your letters about my old friend the Scotch Sergeant. No, I have never heard from him since I came over here, and I often wonder where and how he is. But I must close this now. The mail is going out in a few minutes and I want to get this off today. You have all my love, dearest Hazel. The dugout is rainproof, but the house is not, and so when the weather is nice we live in the house, and when it rains, we go to the dugout and we live in the dugout most of the time. The rats are a pest here. They are so daring. They come out of their holes and sit and look at you, and at night they just play tag on our bunks. Up here we hang our sack up every night. Not for Santa but to keep the rats from eating. I hate rats, don’t you? There isn’t a thing going on up here and I haven’t seen a newspaper for over two weeks and I don’t know what is going on in the world. I am going down to the Company tomorrow and I am going to try and get a paper in St. Mihiel. When we first came up here we could get papers at Verdun, but the Y.M.C.A. that was there has moved. I have been quite busy today. Took a bath this morning (in a bucket) and this afternoon I washed and boiled my clothing, and I darned a pair of socks that the rats chewed up for me a few nights ago. I have an idea that I should make an ideal husband for some nice girl after I get out of this, what do you think about it, dear? We are very anxious for the Peace Conference to get started so we may find out something about how long we will have to stay over here. What is uppermost in every American Soldier’s mind now is when are we going home. I never heard that question asked while the fighting was going on, but I hear it a thousand times a day now. The days are long and monotonous now, and they will be until we find out what we are going to do. I wonder if your cousin Rowland ever came to France. If he didn’t, he has missed the greatest chance in his lifetime. Sweetheart, it has been a wonderful trip and we are all coming home better Americans, and better men, for having made it. I can see a great change in all these men. Why, most of them, their Mother’s won’t know them when they come home. They left home nearly 18 months ago, many of them mere boys just out of school, and they are coming back now as real men, and the finest and cleanest kind of men. I can’t help but think how proud and happy their Mothers will be of them. I am just a litle disappointed that we are not in the Army of Occupation. I would love to see some of the country between here and the Rhine. I have been in Germany but I would love to see Metz or Luckzenburg. Of course, we may get a chance to see them yet. I wanted to get a furlough while I am here and go and see my native country once more, but that has been denied me. An order has just come out saying that no American Soldier can go to a neutral country, even after peace has been signed. But I am not near as anxious to go there as I am to go back to America. You just can’t know, Sweetheart, how I long to see you and hold you in my arms and kiss you and tell you how dearly I love you. Will that day ever come? Yes. I am sure it will, and I can’t believe it is very far away. And when I do come back to you, darling, I will never, never leave you. But Sweetheart, I can’t think of anything more to write this time. I wanted to write you a good letter, but I feel that I have failed. But truly, I will try and do better next time. I send you all my love, my dearest Hazel. It rains day and night, and in the fields the mud and water is hip deep. But they have fine hard rock roads in this country and they keep them in repair, or we would never get anywhere. It was these good roads that were the main factor in us winning the war. Sweetheart, I don’t know of a thing to write about. This is a mighty poor letter, but really, I will try and do better next time. I will close this now. With all my love to you, my dearest Hazel. Always your own, Carl P.S. Always your own Carl Monfort, Le Mans Area, France March 15, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I know you will think that I have forgotten you entirely because I have written you so litle lately, but dear, I have been so very busy the last 3 weeks. When I was on furlough, I was so very busy. You see, I only had 3 days and there were so many interesting places to see, and I neglected to write you, and then when I came back, the Divisional Post Office had moved. But I mailed you a short letter and just a lot of post cards, and a handbag, and I hope it will all come through in good shape. I came all the way from Aulnois in an ambulance and we had a mighty fine trip. It is about 350 miles. It is a pleasure to travel over this country in a car. They have the finest road in the world. And then, you see so much more of the country. I think I have seen more of this country and learned more about the French people in the last 3 weeks than I have in all the time over here. And I realize now that I have lived in the backyard of France ever since I have been over here. We only came through one large town coming down here (Orleans). That is a fine town. I am going to send you a number of postcards from there. And I saw that beautiful statue of Joan of Arc delivering Orleans. But I will tell you all about it when I come to see you. I must tell you about my billet. For the first time in France, I am comfortable. In fact, I am living in style. We are quartered in an old castle, or chateau as they call them over here. It is a beautiful building. It was built in 1416. It is owned by an old couple. I think their title is what they call Baron and Baroness, and there is a cousin of the Baroness living with them. She is 46 years old. I am told they are descendants of Napoleon but they have lost everything but their titles. The Baron and his wife are about 65 years old. I understand that they were very wealthy once, but the Baron lost everything but his house in Monte Carlo. The old couple are quite feeble and you can tell by looking at them that they have lived a very fast life. The two women both smoke cigarettes from morning till night. All the aristocratic women in France smoke cigarettes and the Americans can have anything they have for cigarettes. I don’t understand these French people. They will sell their lives, yes, their souls, for cigarettes or chocolate. But I will tell you more about them when I get home. Let us go back to the chateau. I have a room on the 3rd floor all to myself, and a real bed to sleep in. Can you imagine me sleeping in a real bed dear? After I have slept on everything but a bed for nearly 2 years. The Officers and Sergeants of my Company are all staying here and we are certainly enjoying it. Of course, we don’t know how long we will be here. I understand we are booked to sail about April 10th. It may be sooner and it may be a litle later, but I am almost sure that we will be on American soil by May 10th. I don’t dare predict sooner than that, for I may get disappointed. Sweetheart, I am going to close this now, but I will write you again suite de suite, which is French for real soon. All my love to you, dearest Hazel. Always your own Carl Monfort, France March 20, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I have tried to get a letter off to you for several days. We have been so very busy here lately, standing inspections, cleaning and polishing up every day. All our equipment got badly soiled coming down here and we must be spick and span before we leave this country. There is a slogan out here. It is, ‘Cleanliness is the passport to U.S.A.’, and I just wish you could see the Boys clean up. We haven’t heard anything lately as to when we will sail, but I think there are 2 Divisions on the list ahead of us. But unless the order is changed, I am sure we should sail somewhere around April 10th, and that should bring us out of service about May 20th. So, you see dear, you had better not expect me before June 1st, for if you expect me sooner, you may be disappointed. Sweetheart, you said in one of your letters that I would think I had dropped out of the world when I came down to see you. Well, let me tell you dear, if I can drop out of the world down there, then that is the place I want to come. I want to get away from the world and its noise and turmoil for a while, and I don’t think I will ever go back to the city to live again. We have had real nice weather here up till 4 days ago, but it has rained continually ever since, and we are in mud up to our knees. But we are used to mud. In fact, we would be lost without it. I am going out to see Albert one of these days. I haven’t seen him since Christmas up close to Verdun. He is about 10 miles from here. I am mighty glad to hear that he had a good furlough. I had the best time on my furlough that I ever had. I will never forget that trip, and I have so much to tell you about it when I come home, and I am glad that I went, for I got a different opinion of France and the French people down there. That surely is God’s Country. I only wish I could have spent a week there. There were so many interesting places to see, and I just had to rush through them. But sweetheart, I must close this now. We are going on a march, but I will write you again real soon. I am enclosing some post cards that I gathered on my furlough. I send you all my love, my dearest Hazel. Always your own Carl March 22, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I had another letter from you yesterday dated March 5th and I am going to try to write you a few lines tonight. But really dear, I don’t know what to write about, for our thoughts are filled with going home and it is hard for us to think of anything else. And I wish I could tell you when we were coming, but I can’t, but things are happening pretty fast around here now, and I really believe that the Division should be on the water by the 15th of April. The 26th Division are leaving here now, and according to Stars-Stripes, the 42nd are next. But I do believe that we will leave here ahead of them, for they are in Germany and they haven’t left there yet. We are to have our final inspection here tomorrow, and if they have the ships, I really think we will leave here in the next 10 days. We are having the typical French weather again. Rain every day, but we can keep dry now. Nearly every man in the Company is sleeping in a real bed. We had a dance here yesterday. 20 Y.M.C.A. girls came out from Le Mans and we cleared the big dining room in the parlor and we had a fine time. The Y.M.C.A. has been very careful in selecting the girls they are sending over here. They are the finest girls a man could wish to meet, and they are doing a great deal of good here now. I don’t see how they stand it. They go around and dance with the Boys every afternoon and night and it certainly is hard work. Ex Governor Hanley of Indiana was here this morning and spoke to us. He was a fine talker, and we enjoyed him so much. Dearest, I just can’t think of anything more this time, but I will write you again real soon. All my love to you, dearest. Always your own Carl Le Mans Area March 28, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I received two letters from you yesterday dated March 10 and 12th, and I was so glad to have them, and I was so mighty glad to know that the lace reached you in good condition and that you were pleased with it. And you said I had good taste. Well really, dear, I think the credit belongs to a lady from the Red Cross, for I took her advice. Sweetheart, I have been the sickest man you ever saw the last four days, but I am up today feeling much better. We have had another injection of anti-typhoid serum, and it nearly killed me. In fact, for several days I didn’t care if I lived or not. We used to get it in 3 injections 7 days apart, but this time they gave it all to us in one and it put us all to bed. But we are coming out of it in good shape and we will all be well again in a day or two. I am glad that they have given me my last injection. I get so awful sick every time they give it to me. I think the Division will start for St. Nazaire about April 2nd or 3rd and we should get away from here about the 9th. And I believe we will be on the water on or before the 15th, and how we long for the day to come when we walk down the gang plank and set foot on American soil once more. Dearest, there isn’t a thing to write about and I will close this. I am enclosing a coy of the Swab. You have all my love, dearest. Always your own Carl St. Nazaire, France April 7, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I didn’t think I would have another opportunity to write you from France. We thought we would sail today, but our ship didn’t come in and I don’t know how soon it will come in, but we are enjoying ourselves here. This is the cleanest and most perfect camp I have ever been in. My barrack is just a block from the beach, and our mess hall is right on the beach, and I spend a great deal of my time down there watching the tide come in and go out. There are many interesting litle animals and fishes wash ashore, but the most interesting animals to us are the ships that come in to port that will carry us back to Amerique suite de suite. I am enclosing a litle paper they are putting out here. I will write you again in a day or two if we don’t sail. All my love to you, dearest. Your own Carl T. On board ship April 25, 1919 My dearest Hazel, The Y.M.C.A. man has just brought round writing paper and I will try to write you a short letter. We loaded on the boat on the 14th but didn’t leave France till the 16th on account of high sea. We had very rough sailing the first two days out, but since then, we have had fine weather. But I haven’t enjoyed the trip at all. I have been sick all the way over. The food doesn’t agree with me. All food is cooked by steam and I get sick every time I try to eat. This is the 10th day out and we expect to see land tomorrow, and we will go ashore Sunday morning, and I think I will be close to Funstan by the time this letter reaches you. We will land in Newport News and there we will drop all the new men we got in France. Only the old men will go to Funstan. We landed in France with 123 men and we are coming back with 81. I am enclosing a copy of our newspaper and also a picture of the boat. I will write you again as soon as I can. All my love to you dear. No personal note.] Camp Funstan, God’s Country May 5, 1919 My dearest hazel, Arrived here 7 o’clock this a.m. and we are sure glad to be back again. Always your own Carl Camp Funstan, Kansas May 6, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I have just received your letter of May 4th, and I was so very glad to hear from you. Again, it seems an awful long time since I have had a letter from you. Yes, dear, I too wished that you could have been in Kansas City when we arrived in to Kansas City, for I wanted to see you worse than anyone else. Will it make you jealous, dear, when I tell you that I was hugged and kissed by many women and girls? And many of them I had never seen before. One girl told me she couldn’t find her soldier and she just had to kiss some soldier. We had hoped to be able to leave here tomorrow, but I hardly think we will get away from here until Thrusday evening. We are not doing a thing here, just sitting around waiting for our final paper, and the days are so awful long. We have had our physical, vaccinations, and we are waiting for our discharges to be made out. The machine gun battalions from Joplin, Webb City, and Carthage left here today, and the ammunition train is going out early in the morning. If I get away from here Thrusday evening as I hope I will, I am going to spend a few days in Kansas City and go to church there Sunday, and then I expect to go to St. Joseph the first part of next week. And after that, I am coming down to see you, dear, but I am afraid you won’t know me when I come. My friends tell me that I have changed so much and I know I have. I have aged at least ten years and I am going to ask you to come up to Joplin and meet me when I come down there. I am going to ask you to write and tell me that you will come up to meet me and what time the train from Seneca comes in to Joplin, and I will be there to meet you. Sweetheart, you asked me to write you a long letter, and I would like to, but I can’t. I am so restless and there is such confusion here. Everybody is busy preparing to leave, and I am going to close this now, and I will write you again real soon. I send you all my love. Your Carl c/o General Delivery Kansas City, Mo

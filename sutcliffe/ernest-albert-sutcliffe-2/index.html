The Fascination of Genealogy

(or perhaps… Jean-eology) When people speak of “the fascination of genealogy,” they usually mean tracing their family roots, uncovering noble ancestors, or discovering that great-great-uncle Silas was wanted in three states. But today, let’s follow a different branch of the family tree — one made entirely of denim. Welcome, dear reader, to the study of jean-eology: the science of tracing your blue-jean ancestry. Blue jeans, like some distant cousin you didn’t know you had, started humble and ended up world-famous. In the 1850s, Levi Strauss, a Bavarian immigrant, brought sturdy canvas to California hoping to make tents for miners. The miners said, “Forget the tents—our pants keep quitting on us.” Enter Jacob Davis, a tailor with a rivet fetish, and together they created the first pair of indestructible trousers. Thus began the first family line of jean-eology: the Leviathans of Levi’s. At first, denim was strictly workwear — miners, cowboys, railroad men. The early records of jean-eology show few social climbers in this branch of the tree. But by the mid-20th century, the line had diversified. Marlon Brando and James Dean gave birth to the Rebellious Teenager sub-species, while ranch hands and grease monkeys continued the Sturdy Practical line. Parents, of course, denounced both as dangerous mutations. By the 1980s, jean-eology entered its loud adolescence. Stone-washing, acid-washing, and the mysterious “mom jean” era followed. In the 1990s, the Ripped and Angsty strain appeared, eventually mating with High Fashion to produce the rare but expensive Designer Distressed. Modern jean-eologists can trace these hybrid species through their unique rivet patterns and mysterious fade marks. Today, jean-eology has gone global. Skinny jeans dominate some regions; relaxed fits still thrive in others. There are even endangered species—boot-cuts, bell-bottoms, and anything with a pleat. Yet all share a common ancestor: a pair of indigo-dyed pants that refused to die. So yes, the fascination of genealogy is real—but the fascination of jean-eology may be even greater. Because unlike your relatives, your jeans never forget where they came from. They just get tighter every Thanksgiving.

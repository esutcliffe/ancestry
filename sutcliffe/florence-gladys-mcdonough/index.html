Florence Gladys McDonough Tracy

Mother, Daughter, Grandmother: The Quiet Legacy of Florence Tracy

Florence Gladys McDonough was born on December 5, 1908, in St. Martins East, Saint John County, New Brunswick, Canada. She was the daughter of William John McDonough and Margaret Ann “Maggie” Miller, both age forty-two at the time of her birth. St. Martins was a small coastal community on the Bay of Fundy, known for its shipbuilding heritage, red sandstone cliffs, and the rhythm of its powerful tides. It was there, amid the maritime scenery of her parents’ native province, that Florence’s story began. Her family’s roots were Irish—part of the Scots-Irish migration that had settled along the New Brunswick coast in the nineteenth century. The McDonoughs identified with the Baptist faith, a denomination that flourished among the Protestant Irish communities of the Maritimes. This blend of Irish heritage and New England–style Baptist values shaped Florence’s early life with its emphasis on humility, perseverance, and faith. She was the youngest surviving child in a family of twelve, growing up in a household that had weathered both hardship and devotion. Florence spent her early childhood in St. Martins, surrounded by family, the sea, and the close-knit rhythms of village life. Among her early acquaintances was Hanford Wesley Tracy, a local boy whose family shared the same Irish-Baptist heritage. The two would later reunite in Rhode Island and build a life together. In 1915, at the age of six, Florence immigrated to the United States with her family, settling in Providence, Rhode Island. Like many New Brunswick families of the era, the McDonoughs were drawn south by the promise of steady work in the thriving textile and manufacturing industries of southern New England. Providence, a bustling mill city filled with immigrant neighborhoods, would remain Florence’s home for most of her life. Florence graduated from high school, an achievement that reflected both her ambition and her family’s belief in education as a means to stability and advancement. On September 9, 1929, she married Hanford Wesley Tracy in Providence, renewing the childhood connection that began years earlier in their seaside hometown. The couple settled in the Providence–Cranston area, where Hanford built a career in the refrigeration and ice industry. Together, they raised two daughters: Phyllis Jean (born 1930) and Maitland F. (born 1936). Census records show the family living in Cranston during 1935, 1940, and 1950, reflecting the stable home they maintained through the Great Depression and World War II. In 1941, Florence became a naturalized U.S. citizen, formalizing the bond she had already built with her adopted country. Her adult life was defined by faith, family, and quiet resilience. She endured the loss of her husband, Hanford Wesley Tracy, who died in August 1962 after a year-long battle with cancer. Florence, then fifty-three years old, cared for him tenderly throughout his illness, and his passing marked a profound turning point in her life. After Hanford’s death, she became increasingly reclusive, finding comfort in the quiet familiarity of home and in the company of those she loved most—her two daughters and her beloved granddaughter, with whom she shared a special closeness. In her later years, Florence faced the challenges of Alzheimer’s disease, which gradually diminished her memory and independence. She never maintained a home in Warwick but was eventually moved to a nursing facility there, where she lived out her final years with care and dignity. Florence passed away in Warwick, Rhode Island, on February 19, 1995, at the age of eighty-six, and was laid to rest at Pawtuxet Memorial Park (Section NPC,H, Lot 22, Grave 6). Her life bridged two countries and nearly the entire span of the twentieth century. Born in a maritime village of Canada and matured amid the industrial neighborhoods of Rhode Island, Florence’s story mirrors that of countless New England families whose roots stretch across the Bay of Fundy. Through faith, perseverance, and love of family, she carried forward the quiet strength of her Irish-Maritime heritage.

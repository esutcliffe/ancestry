Florence Gladys McDonough Tracy (1908–1995)

From the Bay of Fundy to Rhode Island

Let me tell you about Florence Gladys McDonough, a girl from a little village on the Bay of Fundy who grew up to make her home in Rhode Island. She is your great-great-grandmother: her older daughter, Phyllis, was your great-grandmother, and Phyllis’s daughter, Candace, is your grandmother.

A Seaside Beginning Florence was born on December 5, 1908, in St. Martins East, Saint John County, New Brunswick, Canada. Her father, William John McDonough, was a St. Martins man, the son of William McDonough and Martha Tufts. Her mother, Margaret Ann “Maggie” Miller, was born at Grand Lake, New Brunswick, the daughter of Conrad and Bridget A. Miller. William was forty-two when Florence arrived, and Maggie was about forty. St. Martins was a small coastal place on the Bay of Fundy, known for its shipbuilding, its red sandstone cliffs, and the rhythm of its powerful tides. That is where Florence’s story begins, in the province her parents called home. The family’s roots were Irish, part of the Scots-Irish migration that settled along the New Brunswick coast in the nineteenth century. The McDonoughs were Baptists, as the 1901 and 1921 Canadian censuses show, a faith that flourished among the Protestant Irish of the Maritimes. That mix of Irish heritage and New England–style Baptist values shaped her early years, with its emphasis on humility, perseverance, and faith. Florence was one of at least eleven McDonough children, and the youngest to grow up. Her baby brother Weldon, born in 1911, lived only a few months, and her sister Olive died in 1921 at sixteen. It was a household that had known both hardship and devotion. Her early childhood was spent in St. Martins, among family, the sea, and the close-knit rhythms of village life. One of the children she knew there was Hanford Wesley Tracy, a local boy whose family belonged to the Church of England. The two of them would meet again in Rhode Island and build a life together. Between Two Homes By 1915, when Florence was six, the family had gone south to Providence, Rhode Island, where the state census of that year finds them. Like many New Brunswick families of the time, the McDonoughs were drawn by the promise of steady work in the textile mills and factories of southern New England. But they did not stay. By 1921 they were back in St. Martins, and the Canadian census that year lists Florence, then twelve, at home by the Bay of Fundy once more. In September 1923, Florence seems to have made the trip again, sailing from Saint John and entering the United States through Boston. That crossing comes to us from a naturalization index rather than a ship’s list, so the details may be a little loose. The next year, on November 17, 1924, her father died back in St. Martins. Providence, a busy mill city full of immigrant neighborhoods, would be Florence’s home for most of her grown-up life. Florence graduated from high school, something that says a good deal about her own drive and about her family’s belief that education was the road to a steadier life. In September 1929, she married Hanford Wesley Tracy in Providence, picking up the friendship that had begun years before in their seaside hometown. Conflicting records: pages on this site give two different days in September 1929 (the 9th and the 12th), and no marriage record has been found yet. Raising a Family Florence and Hanford settled in the Providence–Cranston area, where Hanford made his career in the refrigeration and ice business. They raised two daughters: Phyllis Jean, born in 1930, and Maitland F., born in 1936. The census finds the family in Cranston in 1935, 1940, and 1950, a steady home kept together through the Great Depression and the Second World War. In 1941, Florence became a naturalized U.S. citizen, making official the bond she had long since built with her adopted country. Faith, family, and a quiet kind of resilience were at the center of her grown-up life. Loss and Later Years In August 1962, Florence lost Hanford after a year-long fight with cancer. She was fifty-three, and she cared for him tenderly all through his illness. His death was a turning point for her. Afterward she kept more and more to herself, finding comfort in the familiar quiet of home and in the people she loved most: her two daughters and her beloved granddaughter, with whom she shared a special closeness. In her later years, Florence lived with Alzheimer’s disease, which slowly took away her memory and her independence. She never kept a home in Warwick, but she was eventually moved to a nursing facility there, where she spent her last years cared for and treated with dignity. Florence died in Warwick, Rhode Island, on February 19, 1995, at the age of eighty-six. She was laid to rest at Pawtuxet Memorial Park (Section NPC,H, Lot 22, Grave 6). Her life bridged two countries and nearly all of the twentieth century. Born in a Canadian maritime village and grown up among the mill neighborhoods of Rhode Island, Florence lived a story like that of so many New England families whose roots stretch back across the Bay of Fundy. Through faith, perseverance, and love of family, she carried forward the quiet strength of her Irish-Maritime heritage, and some of it came down to you.

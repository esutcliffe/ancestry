Chapter 3 Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma August 28, 1917 – April 24, 1918 Fort Sill,Oklahoma August 29, 1917 Dear Hazel, I have been looking for a letter from you for a day or so. The mail service is not very good here yet, and your letter is evidently being held up somewhere, but I have a litle time tonight and I am going to utilize it by sending you a few lines. We arrived here last Sunday evening in what I consider the most forsaken country I have ever seen. Our camp here is in a bad condition. In fact, the government was not prepared to take care of us. There is about 6,000 troops here now and we haven’t any water here. We got hold of enough water today to wash our face, but we haven’t been able to get a bath yet. We had no water to drink the first 2 days we were here, but the government is working hard every day to get the pipelines laid out and I think things will adjust themselves in a few days. However, it is pretty hard on the Boys not to be able to get water to bathe in in this hot and dusty country. I am glad you received the flowers I sent you and I am very glad that you enjoyed them. I knew you would. I sent you a letter a few days before I left Riley and I also sent you a picture. Did you get it? Well, Hazel, we are now busy receiving our final instruction and training for foreign service, and I really think we shall see some real service before very long. And I wonder how we shall adjust ourselves to the new and strange conditions over there. We are receiving the final inoculation now for typhoid fever and cholera and we are being issued clothing and equipment for trench work. But we have just received orders to move our camp to another locality and I will have to close for this time, but I am going to look for a long letter from you soon. You won’t disappoint me, will you? I am as ever Your Soldier Brother Carl Fort Sill, Oklahoma September 1, 1917 Dear Hazel, I have been waiting for a letter from you for some time, but haven’t heard a thing from you for about 10 days. We left instructions with the postal authorities in Fort Riley to forward our mail to us as we were not sure what our address would be, but up to this time we haven’t received any mail from there and consequently we are all feeling somewhat blue. However, we are getting settled down here and conditions are gradually improving. I have been swimming this afternoon. There is a fine litle lake about 5 miles from our camp. I rode my horse over there. I took several pictures on the way and I am going to send you one of each later on. There is really some beautiful scenery around here. I am going to take a trip to the Indian Reservation tomorrow. I am told there is about 30,000 Comanche Indians living on the Reservation. We see many of them every day. The come in and watch us drill. They still wear their native costumes here. Another very interesting sight here is the aviation service. They have 60 airplanes here now and it is not unusual to see from 15 to 25 up in the air at one time. I have a friend here in the aviation section who has promised to take me out on a trip some time next week but I am not sure that I want to go up with him. Next week we are going to get our final inoculation for typhoid fever. It is called para typhoid, and given to all soldiers before going in to foreign service. The general impression now seems to be that we will go to France sometime in November. The Oklahoma ambulance company and field hospital will leave here this evening for Garden City, N.Y. from where they will sail for Europe soon and I wish we could go with them. I am getting very anxious to go, but I am going to bed now. It is 10 o’clock and I will finish this in the morning. Sunday a.m. I was woke up this morning at 6 o’clock by the 2nd Mo’s hound dog and say, they have a real hound dawg this year. Co. A of the 2nd arrived here some time early this morning and we heard them coming for a mile. The dog led the march from station to the camp. I expect the rest of the Missouri and Kansas Boys will be coming in pretty fast from now on. I understand they have 10,000 Boys at Nevada, Mo. Ready to come. But I think I will have to close for this time. I am going over to the service at the Y.M.C.A. this morning. Hoping this will find you well and happy and hoping that I will get a long letter from you real soon. I remain as ever Carl P.S. Excuse the soiled paper. We are living in dust here and it is impossible to keep it clean. I am enclosing a few pictures I took at Ft. Riley. [Note: 13 photographs of Ft. Riley camp life] Fort Sill, Olahoma September 3, 1917 [Note: Hazel has marked this letter “special” on the envelope] Dear Hazel, I received your letter from Ft. Riley yesterday, and your letter of Sep 1st came today, and I was so glad to hear from you. I had been looking for mail from you for several days. I mailed you a letter last night and I don’t know if I can find much to write about this time, but this is a holiday and I have ample time and I shall try to send you a few lines. I went up to the Wichita Mountains this morning and watched the field artillery practice shrapnel firing with 3 inch guns. There was 96 cannons out there and it was very interesting. They shoot the shells out of the guns and you can see them go through the air several miles before the explode. They can be set to explode anywhere from ½ mile to 5 miles from where the guns are, and when they explode they scatter fragments of steel over a large area. But Hazel, I am glad that I do not belong to the fighting branch of the service. It gives me a great deal of pleasure to be able to serve in a branch where I can help to relieve the suffering. You wonder how long we will be here. Well, Hazel, it is hard to say, but I can not help but believe that we will be in Europe by Christmas and if it is at all possible for me to get away for a week then I am coming to see you before I go, for of course, none of us can be sure of coming back. Surely a good many of us will not be here to answer roll call after the war, but as I have told you before, I have no fear for the outcome. Let me quote you a part of a letter that a good friend of my Mother sent me some time ago. She writes, “I wish some woman, A Mother, would write a letter than can be nailed up somewhere in the lounging places of the men. I have tried. I find I can not write that letter to a million men. It assumes a right I do not possess, but if I were writing it, I should say something like this. When things get a litle strained over there, as they will now and then, we just want you to remember this. We are here. All of us. Solidly behind you. We love you and believe in you. You have entered upon a new life that is always strange, and often terrible. Home and things at home seems very far away. But we are praying that some day you are coming back to us and the old familiar things again. Remember this: if prayers, high hope and sleepless nights avail, you are coming back to us to take up life again where you left off. We know things are different over there. War levels many barriers. The very proximity of Death makes Life seem a thing to be lived fully. War itself is a great adventure. Here at home, things go on in the same old way. With this difference our eyes are strained to the time when you are coming back. You will remember that, won’t you, when things get a bit strong over there? We want you to come back. God only knows how we want that. But we want you to come back the better for having gone. The finer, the stronger, for having fought for a great cause. We want you whole of body if so ordered, but whole of soul by your own strength.” And then she adds, “Carl, I have never preached to you. You know I am not a preacher, but I am asking one thing of you. :A clean slate: I want you to come home from this school of war with a clean slate, and if in the fullness of a purpose we do not understand, you are not to come back to us here but must meet us again in a land where there is no more wars but everlasting peace, then that clean slate of yours will be a fine thing to take along over there.” I consider that a fine piece of advice and I am taking it with me. Hazel, I remember as a child when I went out to play. Mother always warned me not to get hurt. That sounds funny now, but there are so many ways to get hurt and particular so in the Army. And cigarette smoking is one of them. No, I do not smoke, Hazel. I am on of the 10 out of 100 soldiers who does not smoke. Cigarettes, liquor, and vice are the 3 evil influences we have to fight. Of course, it is only the younger class that falls for them. We are fighting them all hard, and I think with liquor barred from the Army now, we are going to beat the other two. As I see the young men coming in to camp by the hundreds every day now, I wonder what kind of soldiers they are going to make, and what kind of men they are going to make, and I hope they will all come back home the better for their experience. I think that of all the experiences and adventures I have had in my life this far, those I have gleaned from my 14 months in the Army have had the most profound effect in shaping my destiny. The recruiting posters tells us that a year in the Army will teach our Boys courage. It will. It will also teach us how to die, and in teaching us this, it will teach us how to live. We learn how to obey in order that in the latter Battle of Life we may know how to command. It teaches us democracy and if after we have learned to defend our country and ourselves, we are called upon to make practical use of our education for the benefit of posterity. We learn tenderness and manliness and how to sacrifice without thinking about it. Pacifists argue to the contrary, but if anybody can show me a man who has seen service and is a Pacifist and denies my statement then I am willing to take the short end of a bet that his record in the War Department shows him on the sick report when he wasn’t in the guardhouse for being a dirty, careless, inefficient soldier. Military service in the U.S. Army is a real job with variegated details. Many of them unpleasant to some natures, but none of them degrading. And the Army is the finest school in the world for teaching a boy courage, cleanliness, self reliance, and efficiency. But Hazel, I must close now. How is this for a long letter? It is almost supper time now. I remain as ever, Your big but not handsome soldier brother, Carl P.S. May I look for a long, long letter real soon? I did not start out to write a book. Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma September 9, 1917 Dear Hazel, You have been very good to me to answer my letters so soon. I received your letter of the 5th this morning and your last letter came this afternoon, and this has been a happy day for me, for I enjoy your letters so much. We moved to our new camp yesterday and we have just completed our camp and gotten cleaned up, and I like it so much better than where we were. I think we have one of the nicest locations on the Reservation. We are camping at the foot of the Wichita Mountains and I think I shall like it very well here after we get settled down. It takes about 2 weeks to get located in a new camp. There is so many litle things to fix up. I have washed all my clothing today except thos I have on. We have no laundry here and we have to do our own washing, but then, I am used to it and I don’t mind. That is one of the many duties that the new soldiers will have to learn when they get in to the Army. I took a ride over the Reservation and I went part ways up in the mountains and took a good many pictures and I will send you some of them later on. At the present, I haven’t a picture of my horse, but I will take one and you shall get one before I go to France. I am very glad that you enjoy the pictures I send you. I am going to try to send you a good many pictures from here for there really is some beautiful scenery around. How I wish you could see the country around here. I saw many interesting sights out on the Indian Reservation Sunday, but unfortunately, I ran out of films before I got out there. I had lunch with a family of them, but they certainly are a peculiar tribe. This family I visited has a grown son and daughter who had both graduated from the college in Lawton. They were well educated but still they remain out on the Reservation and live like animals. They don’t seem to [have] any ambition at all. I venture to say that there are hundreds of young Indians around here who have a much better education than I have ever been able to get, laying around and the government has to support them. Citizens in Lawton tells me that they will come to the city and get a position and work for a week or two, but that is their limit. They then discard their civilian clothing, grab their blankets, and go back to the wild again. They live principally from hunting and fishing. Some of them have a few hungry looking cattle and sheep, and each family has at least a dozen dogs. But they are certainly an interesting race to study. Hazel, it is very kind of you to think of my needs in France. But at the present time, you better not start on anything. In fact, I think about the only thing I will need is a pair of heavy woolen socks. It is true, there are many things we could use, but we will not be allowed to carry but 50 pounds with us. So you see, we won’t be able to carry much. The transportation of an army is a big item and they [are] cutting down our baggage to the minimum. But I will tell you, Hazel, if you will knit me a pair of heavy socks or two later on, I would be very glad to have them, but I want you to allow me to buy the material. I don’t feel that I should put you to any expense. You have done so much for me. I am going over and take a picture of the Houn Dawg tomorrow. I want you to see what he looks like. I think they will manage to store him away somewhere until they get aboard the ship. The Boys all say that if he can’t go with them, why, the 2nd will stay at home. Well, Hazel, I will close for this time. The Boys are pretty loud here tonight and it is hard to write. The Boys are so pleased with our new camp they are having a celebration of their own. They are having a ghost parade right now and I think it is going to wind up with a shirt tail parade. I will be looking for another long letter real soo. I remain as ever Carl Fort Sill, Oklahoma September 14, 1917 Dear Hazel, Your dear letter came yesterday and, as usual, I was so glad to hear from you. And I tried so hard to answer your letter last night but I have been so sick the last 2 days that I had to give it up. However, I am feeling somewhat better this morning but I am far from being well yet. We have received one of 3 inoculations against typhoid fever and malaria, and we were also vaccinated against smallpox, and it has made us all pretty sick. And a man is in a hard shape when he takes sick in military camp, as things are not very convenient. But I am trying to see the bright side of life anyway. You ask me how we manage to iron our clothing. Well, we don’t iron them when we are in the field. We are lucky to get them washed. We had a sad accident here a few days ago. A young man in the aviation section was burned to death. His machine caught fire 3,000 feet in the air. I shall never forget the sight of that machine plunging to the gound. The man was burned beyond recognition when the machine reached the ground. He lived 6 hours after, but I suppose we shall see many similar sights before this war is over. He was a fine young man, too. Just graduated from Harvard. We escorted his body to the station in a body [bag?]. His home is Minneapolis, Minn. Hazel, I haven’t any pictures to send you this time. I did not get the Houn Dawg’s picture Sunday. He had gone out for a walk when I got over there. The doctor has just left my tent and he ordered me to stop writing at once and go right back to bed, and I will have to stop. Hazel, I read the K.C. Star and that is about all I can find time for now. I don’t always find time to do that and I just don’t need a thing here (except your company). How I wish I could see you. I am sure we could have some fine times together. You will excuse my short letter this time, won’t you? For really, I am in no shape to write. I will look for a letter from you soon. I remain Yours as ever Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma September 18, 1917 [Note: Picture postcard of Medicine Park with “X” over hill] Just a line to tell you we are getting along fine. I went to the top of this mountain yesterday. It is 1 ½ mile from bottom to top. I was pretty tired when I got back to camp. Regards from Carl September 20, 1917 Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma Dear Hazel, I think you are the dearest litle girl for writing me such a nice long letter. I have enjoyed it so much and I have also enjoyed our trip to the country very much, only I was disappointed when I got through reading and realizing it was only an imaginary trip. Yes, I am feeling fine again but we are to get our second inoculation tomorrow. We get 3 inoculations 7 days apart. They are called typhoid, para typhoid, and triple typhoid. My smallpox vaccine did not take and I am to have another vaccination in the morning. We will be through in about 2 weeks. Uncle Sam believes in preventing the diseases rather than curing them. Well, Hazel, judging form general indications, we will not be here very long now. Major General Wright left here a few days ago for Hoboken, New Jersey. He left orders here to have all troops here ready in 3 weeks. The Major General will sail for France next week, and while we have no official information, still I believe that he has gone to make arrangements for this Division. Of course, the entire Division is not here yet and chances are good many of the organizations will go direct from their home stations to the point of embarkation. A number of French officers arrived here today direct from France. They will act in advisory capacity with the artillery and aviation section. They wear such peculiar uniforms. Red trousers and blue coats, but they are sure fine soldiers and I think we can learn a great deal from them about modern warfare. Hazel, I am sending you a clipping from the Lawton Sunday paper. Now remember, Hazel, this is not a hint to you to send me any of those things mentioned there because really, I do not need anything that I can not get here. But I think it is a splendid article and every word is true, and I wanted you to read it. [Enclosed newspaper clipping “The Soldier’s Best Moral Influence is the Girl He Left Behind Him.”] Friday a.m. I am in charge of our camp this morning, and our company has gone on a litle hike. They will be back by noon and this afternoon we will get our inoculation and how we wish it was over. We are now passing through the most critical part of our training period. The newness of soldiering has worn off and the Boys are just beginning to realize that they are here to learn to soldier and not on a excursion trip, and it seems hard on a good many of them who has always been used to have their own way. The training is gradually getting harder, the inoculation and vaccination are making things more disagreeable, and the majority of the men are cranky and grouchy. All furloughs has been discontinued and it seems hard for the Boys to get used to conditions. However, I think another month of hard training will get them used to conditions and they will take things as they come. What seems to be hardest on them now is the fact that they can not go home. But they will have to get used to it. We are at war now, and this is no time to howl. The man who obeys his orders and takes things as they come to be is the soldier, and I am trying my best to be a good soldier. It is hard now, but in time to come, I hope to be able to look back to the past with pride, and in learning to be a good soldier, I am learning to be a better man. And I think the effort is well worth the price. But I have duties to perform now and must close. I will look for that nice long letter soon. I remain as ever yours Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma September 26, 1917 [Note: 1 photo of Carl with “Houn Dawg”, 3 photos of “Lady”, 8 photos of Wichita Mountains and scenery around Camp Doniphan (Photos 1 through 12).] Dear Hazel, That nice long letter from you came today and I was so glad to get it, and I shall not let you wait at all for an answer. You shall have it as soon as I can get it to you. About a long, long letter I do not know, but I will try. Hazel, dear, I would be very lonely here did I not have your letters to help me. You have been and you are now an inspiration to me, and you are giving me courage to live a better life and work for better things. If I could only explain to you how much your letters and your friendship means to me, I would be happy. It has not been my good luck to have a sister. Someone to go to in my lonely hours. Someone to help me and guide me through the critical part of my life. But you have filled that place so nicely and in this connection, let me say that I have always had the greatest respect and admiration for you. Your letters has never been put on display in this camp and they never will. I have guarded your letters and your name here as if you were my very own sister. I have always tried to treat you as such. No doubt that some of our Boys here have had a good deal of fun at the expense of some of the girls from Seneca, but I have never been able to enjoy that kind of sport. I should not wish to see my own sister treated that way, and much less treat another man’s sister. So I am thankful for having such a friend as you have been to me and in the future, do not hesitate to be frank with me, for I shall never take advantage of you. I have been exceedingly busy here lately. I have now 348 horses and mules under my supervision and it is not a small undertaking to see that they are cared for properly. The Missouri and Kansas Boys of the National Guard are coming in fast now. 6,000 have come in this week and I think now the entire Division will be here by October 5th. We are now organizing what is called a Sanitary Train, which consists of 4 ambulance companies and 4 field hospitals and a headquarters company. After we are organized, I think we will be known as Ambulance Co #1, 110th Sanitary Train, 35th Division, U.S.A. If we are #1, then we will lose our horses and mules and be given motor ambulances. No. 1 and 3 are motor companies, 2 and 4 animal drawn. And Hazel, I don’t know what to do. It will almost break my heart to give up my horse. I will have an opportunity to transfer to another company. In fact, I have been asked to but I also hate to leave my old organization and friends. But I will find some way out of it, but that is a part of war. No attention can be paid to your personal feeling and sympathy, and the best of friends must part sooner or later. Hazel, do you remember I told you a short time ago I went up in the mountains Kodakking? I am sending you a fe pictures I took up there and around Medicine Lake, and I think I had pretty good luck, don’t you? I will tell you what I am going to do, Hazel. I am going to send you a print of every picture I take while I am in the service and then some day whent this terrible war is over, then I am coming to visit you (that is, if you will let me.) And then I will tell you all about my trip and where the pictures were taken. Every picture has a story. Say, won’t that be fine! I am also enclosing a picture of myself with the famous Houn Dawg and also one of our own mascot, Lady. I don’t know if I ever told you about her. We brought her back with us from the Mexican border, and we are very proud of her. Some of our Boys has fought many a hard fight over her. If anyone wants to kick on Lady, they have 150 men in the ambulance company to fight. It is very cold here tonight. Thousands of men are shivering here. They haven’t enough blankes to keep them warm, and we are not able to get them from the government. Us men who were on the border are fortunate enough to have plenty of woolen clothing and blankets, but the new men are suffering. I am amused now at the old Pacifists who used to say that we could raise and army of a million men over night. Well, maybe we can, but those men must be fed, clothed and equipped, then they must be organized and then they must be trained and hardened up to stand all kinds of hardship. And they must be taught to take care of themselves under the hardest condition. Sleeping out in the open in all kinds of weather. The wind is blowing 60 miles an hour here now and the dust is blowing right through our tents. If a man was to come out here from civil life tonight and live with us tonight, I am afraid he would freeze to death before morning. It takes time to harden up a soldier. It can not be done in a day or two. So you can see what an undertaking it is to raise an army. We may be able to raise a man over night, but it reqires training to make a soldier and a man is not a soldier because he wears a uniform. But I will close for this time. It is getting late and I am getting cold, and I must bid you a good night. I remain as ever your Soldier Brother, Carl Write soon please. Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma October 2, 1917 Dear Hazel, Your letter came today and I was so glad to hear from you, and I am glad that you enjoyed my letter and the pictures. The weather has been very disagreeable here the last 4 dyas. We have had an awful gale here. The wind has been blowing at the rate of 60 miles an hour constantly. We are eating dust and we are sleeping in dust, and of course, it is pretty hard for a Missourian to get used to it. We had our final typhoid inoculation a few days ago and I am surely glad we are through with it. My right arm is still sore from it. Tomorrow we will have our final physical examination for tuberculosis. They have a board of specialists here examining every man here, and they are weeding out all men who shows any symptoms of tuberculosis. It seems the doctors missed a few men in the first examination, and the War Department are not taking any chances. So far they have found at an average of one man in 200 who shows symptoms in greater or less degrees. Hazel, you would be surprised to see how careful we are guarded against contagious diseases, and especially so in the medical department. In fact, many a man is turned down in this branch of service and accepted in other branches. We have 150 of the finest men I have ever seen physically, mentally and morally. We were recruited to war strength almost before anybody else had started. As a result, we obtained the very best of men and I don’t think we shall lose a man. We had a few undesirable men with us on the border last year, but we have gotten rid of them. Do you remember you asked me about a Fred Rusk? Well, he was given a dishonorable discharge for the good of the service. I think that is the severest punishment that a man can receive. The Missouri-Kansas Boys are coming in fast now. I think we have about 25,000 men in camp now. There is 4 ambulance companies and 4 filed hospitals here now, and as soon as they all get organized then we will be known as the 110th Sanitary Train, 35th Division. And I think we will be Ambulance Company #1. I wonder, Hazel, if you can imagine what a task it is to organize and train an army, and I wish you could see the activity here. It is beyond description. On the ground you see thousands upon thousands of soldiers practicing. Hundreds of heavy army motor trucks and today I counted 42 airships and 7 observation balloons in the air at one time. Do you think you can draw a picture in your mind of what a concentration camp looks like? The Houn Dawg Band is playing at the Y.M. tonight, and it sounds good to hear them again. Yes, in a way, I am really sorry to see the consolidation of the State Regiment but I presume the War Department knows what is best. But Hazel, I just can’t find anything to write about tonight and I am going to take a shower bath and go to bed and try my luck in the morning. Wednesday evening I have tried hard all day to get a few minutes to finish this letter but it has just been one thing after another all day long. The horses got scared last night and there was a stampede in the corral, and a number of them were injured and it has kept me busy all day giving them medical attention. Hazel, I am afraid now that I will have to part with my horse. Everything seems to indicate that we will be given motor ambulances, and with the motorized troops, sergeants ride motor cycles and it is going to be pretty hard on me, for I am one of those old fashioned kind of men who loves and horse. In fact, I love all animals, but I will have to live it down. Hazel, I am just wishing tonight that I had a small box of that nice homemade candy. I can still taste that candy you sent me last year. Would it be asking you too much to send me just a small box sometimes when you make candy? You know, I can buy all kinds of candy here, but it doesn’t taste anything like your candy. But I must close for this time. I have another letter to write tonight. I will look for a letter from you real soon. I remain as ever Your Soldier Brother Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma October 8, 1917 Dear Hazel, You are just the Dearest litle girl in the world to me to answer my letters so promptly. I was so glad to get your letter today and I am going to be as prompt as you were. Hazel, I am feeling a litle downhearted, for I had to say good by to my horse today. We are to use motor ambulances in this war and to part with my horse seemed to me just like parting with a member of my family. I am going to start tomorrow to attend a school of instruction in the use of the gas masks. There is 2 men from each organization detailed to take the course. After we complete it we will have to teach it to the men in our own organization. The training in all the various branches of modern warfare is now in full swing here, and this is getting to be a busy place. I think the entire Division is here now with the exception of the 3rd Mo Regiment, and there is a possibility of them being kept in their own state for a home guard. I have today received a letter from the War Department asking me to take a 3 months training for a commission beginning in November, and I am turning it down. I know this will seem foolish to you but I have no ambition to become an officer in the Army. In the first place, I shall never follow soldiering as a profession, and second, I feel I can do more good in the medical department. And you have to have a doctor’s diploma to hold a commission in this branch of service. I enlisted in the Army for the good I could do to my country, and not for what I could get out of it. We are having the first spell of real cold weather here. A storm broke loose last night and it is still raging, and there is thousands of Boys shivering in camp tonight. The Boys has not been issued their woolen uniforms or overcoats and there is a good deal of suffering here. Us Boys who were on the border are fortunate. We have our woolen clothing and we are quite comfortable. Yes, I kept the deers in Kansas City until I came away. Then I gave them to Swope Park and they are growing so fast that I hardly knew them when I was in Kansas City in August, but they knew me the minute I called to them and they came running up to me wagging their tails. The candy did not come with your letter but I think it will come in the morning. But I will close for this time and I will write you when the candy comes. I am very sincerely Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma October 9, 1917 Dear Hazel, Your box of candy came today and I thank you so much for it. And I must say that you are some candy maker. It is just the best I have ever tasted. In fact, it seems to me that anything you do, you always do just right, and I have the greatest admiration and respect for you. You strike me as a very accomplished and practical girl and I am so glad that I have come to know you. I am just ashamed of myself now for asking you to send me that candy, but really Hazel, I was so hungry for a litle homemade candy. I took my first lesson today in the use of poison gas and the application of the gas masks, and I can not say that I like it as far as I have gone. But then, anything new and strange always seems hard at the beginning. I am going up in an observation balloon some day this week. I have a friend who is a captain in the observation school, and he has promised to take me up 1,500 feet and then drop me (that is) I will come down in a parachute. The go up 7,000 feet here but my friend will only take me up 1,500 feet to begin with. He has asked me to transfer to that branch of service, but I am not sure that I would make a good airman. Major General Wright, who is commanding this Division, has just come back from the Western Front. He has spent 2 months over there, and he says that we are not yet in shape to go over there. He was down her lecturing to us last Friday and he told us that we must spend every minute of our time preparing ourselves and hardening us up, for he said we are going to fight the best soldiers in the world and the best man is going to win out. But somehow, it hurts me to see you go out in to a world entirely so new to you. Not that I have any fears for you. I am quite sure that you are able to take care of yourself, but I just hate to see you come in contact with the big outer world as I have seen it. Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma January 7, 1918 Dear Hazel, Your letter came today and your helmet reached me a few days ago, and it is just fine. It was a litle large but I put it in hot water and shrunk it, and it fits just fine, and I have had a great deal of comfort out of it already. I have been a litle busier than usual lately. We are making the preliminary preparations for moving. There are so many litle details to look after. When the order comes for this Division to move for oversea duty, everything must be ready to pack at once, and not only that, but every pack box must be a certain size. No larger or smaller. Each box must have just certain things in it, and every box must be labeled and numbered, and the number must be registered. I wonder if you can realize how much work it involves to move an army of 30,000 men and complete equipment across the seas. I can not help but feel that our stay in this country will be short, and I think as soon as we get everything in shape to move that we will get orders sudden and my beliefs are that we won’t know anything about it until we are on the train. A detail of 60 men from the medical department left here today for an unknown destination. The War Department are playing safety first in all troop movements and don’t be at all surprised if some day you get a letter from me from some seaport town. The sanitary conditions are gradually getting better. Only 900 men in the hospital this week, but that is 600 too many. Of course, we have 1,800 out in the isolation camp. As soon as a man gets sick, the rest of the men who were in the tent with him are isolated and sent to a separate camp about 6 miles from here, and they are kept there under observation and with this system, I think we will soon be rid of the dreadful disease. A good many of the Boys are going home to see their parents for the last time before going over, and no doubt, a good many of them will not return again. And while I could get a furlough for 5 days and I would love to go too, but only a few are allowed to go at a time and I feel that I owe it to those men who have Mothers living, and let them go. And I don’t think I will get away while we are here. But Hazel, before I close, let me say that I never find anything in your letters that seems trivial and insignificant to me. I am so interested in you and I just love to get your letters and I don’t know what I would do without them, but there goes Taps. Good night. As always Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma January 15, 1918 Dear Hazel, Your letter of the 10th came yesterday and I would have written you last night, but it was too cold to write. And speaking of snowstorms, well, we have just had one of the worst storms that we have ever seen. It started in last Friday, and it lasted for about 4 days, and while I don’t think that there was any actually suffering here, but it was mighty uncomfortable. The temperature ran from zero to 13 below for 3 days and nights, and I don’t know what I would have done had I not had the nice woolen socks and helmet you made for me. I have had a great deal of comfort out of them already, Hazel. Those socks are the finest ever for this kind of life, and I wish you could realize how I enjoy them. We had about 6 inches of snow and a 60 mile wind here Saturday night, and it has begun to snow again tonight, and this country is sure having its share of severe weather. However, I think the civilian population is suffering as much, if not more, than we are, for we are all pretty well supplied with woolen clothing and blankets now. We were told when we came down here last summer that it never got cold down here and some of the Boys here say that the people here do not very often tell the truth, and I must say that I never have been so cold before as I have been here this winter, and I have lived in Wisconsin where it sometimes goes down to 50 below. And Hazel, I am mighty glad that you only imagine you hear the wind sweeping across the prairie and around our tents. I mean, I am glad that you don’t have to live in a tent in this country. You say you often wonder what my thoughts are and how I feel about going over there, and I am going to tell you exactly as I feel about it. I can see a big job ahead of me, and I can see a life of suffering, hardship, danger, and death, but still I have never undertaken a thing that I was so anxious to get in to as this. And I feel that I must fight to the last, and that is the way that nearly every man feels about it. Of course, we are beginning to realize that many of us will not come back again and we talk it over quite often between ourselves, but none of the Boys seems to worry over it. At least, I haven’t seen any of them ever take it serious. They oftenjoke about it. The speak of it as being bumped off, or going west to push up the daisies, but somehow we just don’t feel serious about it. Every man is anxious to go. We have today received our trench boots and I am wearing them tonight. They are awful heavy things and they remind me so much of the wooden shoes I wore back in the old country when I was a boy. The equipment are rolling in here now by the trainload and I expect that in another week or 10 days, the Division will be completely equipped. I enjoyed the poem so much that you sent me. It was well written and while I was reading it, it came to my mind that many people think that the men in the medical department have chosen that branch of service because it is less dangerous. Statistics shows that the casualties are much greater in this branch than any other branch of the service. Most of our work is done under shell fire and we do not even carry a gun for our own protection, and we do not like to have anybody think that we are hiding under the Red Cross. But it is getting late and my tent is cold and I will go to bed and get warm. Good night. As always, Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma January 21, 1918 Dear Hazel, Your nice letter has just come and I am so glad to hear from you and to know that you enjoyed my last letter, although I fail to see what you find in it that is interesting, for I have never been able to write a decent letter. I have just come back from the base hospital where I have been since Friday. I have been through my first gas attack. I was in the gas chamber instructing men in gas defense, when my mask suddenly sprang a leak and I was overcome by the chlorine gas, but I was carried out in time to be brought out of it. And outside a litle pain in my lungs, I am feeling good now and I will be on duty again in a day or two. We are short on gas masks here and it seems that a few defective masks were handed out, however, nothing serious happened through the mistake and I guess it will cause us to be more careful in the future. Chlorine gas is very poisonous and unless it is pumped out of your lungs at once, it will kill a person in a few hours. Hazel, can you think of a more cruel or inhumane form of warfare that the Germans are using when they pour a lot of deadly gas over the battle field? And particular so in the early part of the war when the allies had no means of defending themselves against it, and thousands of soldiers were killed by gas in the beginning of the war without knowing what ever had happened to them. The modern gas masks that we are using are absolute protection against any gas they have used so far, and the English sergeants here tell me that no man will ever be gassed if he just keeps his ears open for gas alarms and keeps his mask in good condition, and you bet my mask is going to be my best friend when I get over there, for I had a pretty close call here last week. But I am sure that you are sick and tired of gas by this time. Yes, how I would love, Hazel, to be down with you and take a walk in the peaceful country. Just to get away from this war crazy world for a while would be a great relief, but there is a big job ahead of us. Someday when I come back from this war (if I come back) then I am coming down to visit you if you will let me. We used to have the best of times in the winter. We have so many outdoor sports such as skiing, skating, and sleighing, and also driving the reindeers, and while I have done so much better in this country than I could ever have done over there, still there is something that always seems to draw you back to the place where you spent your childhood days. But I could never go back there to live. By the way, I have forgotten to tell you about our mascots of late. The beautiful Houn Dawg died about 2 months ago, and the old 2nd Regiment is still mourning. And Lady, she has 8 of the prettiest pups that you have ever seen and she is the proudest dog that I ever did see. We built her a nice litle house and she will not allow a stranger to come near her house. Any man of our own company can go down and visit her and play with the pups, but no outsider can get close to her house. We are going to ship her back to Kansas City next week and I don’t know how we will get along without her, for it will seem like losing a member of our family. But orders are orders. I do not know yet how soon we will get out of here, but I do know that we will go from here direct to France by way of England, and we are due in France 26 days after we leave here. And Hazel, don’t thank me for those litle things, for if you will only enjoy them as much as I have, then I will be happy. This is Saturday afternoon and the weeks work is over, and it has been a very busy week for us. The Inspector General of the Army has been here and he has more than inspected us. I have stood many a rigid inspection, but I have never stood as rigid an inspection as the one he gave me. He was the most wonderful man I have ever seen, and he was the most efficient man I ever saw. It seemed to be his business to find faults, and wherever he did find them, he did not hesitate to point them out to us. I mailed you a letter last night and you should get it tomorrow. I meant to send you Trench – Camp last night but I forgot to put it in, and I am enclosing it in this letter. I am very glad, dear, to know that the play was such a success, but I knew it would be when you took a part in int, for you always do everything right. Yes, dear, I would love to be down there and go Maying with you. How you do love Nature, don’t you? And I am so glad you do. There are so many people who go through life who never have learned to love or appreciate Nature’s Beauty. I know south Missouri is wonderful at this time of the year. I used to go fishing in the Ozarks. The last trainload of the infantry left this afternoon and we are so anxious to know what is going to happen in the next few days. We all feel sure that our orders should come now within a day or two. Do you remember, dear, how anxious I was on the border when I knew we were going to be ordered home? Well, it is the same way now, only I am more anxious to go over there. Do you know, Hazel, if we stay in England for 2 months and I can get a weeks furlough, I may take a trip over to my native country. I would love to see the place where I spent my boyhood days once more, and I can go there from London in about 40 hours, providing I can go across the North Sea. But dearest Hazel, please don’t think that I was finding faults with your letters. I was not. Your letters has always been just perfect to me. You always write me cheerful and encouraging words to me, but I was afraid you were worrying about me. If in the past my letters has helped you and made you happy, I am very glad. You know, dear, I have always wanted a sister, and when you wrote to me on the border, I found in you just the kind of a girl that I wanted my sister to be, and I was happy. Later on, as I began to find in you the qualities I wanted my future wife to have, I was very miserable, for I don’t think I would ever have told you until I came back had you not asked me. But now, dearest Hazel, I am just the happiest man in the world, for having won the heart of the finest and best girl in the world. But I will close for this time and I will look for another letter from you in a few days. Good night, dearest. With all my love to you from your own Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma April 17, 1918 My dearest Hazel, Your dear letter has just come and as usual, I was so glad to have it. And the candy also came, but the doctor has taken it away from me for the present for I am under medical treatment, but I am hoping to be well again in a day or two and I know I shall enjoy the candy so much. It was awful good of you to send it to me, and you are just the dearest Sweetheart a man ever had. Nearly every man in our Company took sick suddenly Monday night. We had beef hash for supper and whatever caused the trouble, we haven’t been able to find. But we have 65 men sick here in camp and 17 in the base hospital. So you see, dear, our litle army is in bad shape right now, but the Boys are all coming through in good shape and we all hope to be out again in a few days. I am beginning to get hungry now and that is a good sign. I have been on light diet for 2 days but that doesn’t satisfy me any more and I am pleading with the doctor to put me on heavy diet tomorrow. It is possible now that all this sickness will delay us in getting away from here. We would not be able to travel should the order come now. It would be a week or 10 days before we could stand the trip, and now, we are beginning to worry that the order will come before we get well and we will be left behind here. I am glad to know that you received the Journal, and Hazel, dear, are there any other magazines you would like to have/ If there is, you must not hesitate to tell me about it, for I will be only too glad to get them for you. You see, dear, you are the only one I have in this world to show my love and affection to, and you are the world and all to me, and I want you to have anything you wish for. You will tell me, won’t you dear? I am just so proud of you dear, and so happy that I have come to know you, and I want always to do everything in my power to make you happy. Yes, I have often wondered if you at times kept company with any one in your town. Now don’t misunderstand me, dearest. I have never doubted your sincerity and I never will, but I thought that you might keep company with some of your men friends for the sake of companionship, and I could never see that there could be anything wrong in it, and I would not object if you desired, but I have always had the most confidence in you and I trust you and believe in you, and dearest, I am true to you and I will always be true to you. And you are the only girl I correspond with and I am not a bit interested in anyone else. I believe I told you once that I could not amuse myself with many litle love affairs. I come from a very strong race and when we love, we love much. And dearest, you will always have all my love and I want all yours. No indeed dear, you are no slacker. Sweetheart, you are far too broad and too fine for that. Dear, the doctor has just ordered me back to bed and he promised me heavy diet tomorrow, and he said I can have the candy, so I must close for this time. With all my love to you, dearest. I am always Your Carl Camp Doniphan, Oklahoma April 24, 1918 [Note: Hazel’s note on the envelope, “Letter came in a box of flowers from Kansas City”.] Dearest Hazel, When you get this, I think I will be far away from you, but I want you to know that my thoughts and my heart is with you always.

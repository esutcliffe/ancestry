Devin Reed Sutcliffe

Anne's Cess Tree

In the small New England town of Button Hollow, where even the squirrels were rumored to pay property tax, there lived a woman named Anne who owned the most magnificent tree anyone had ever seen. It stood in the center of her yard — wide-armed, deeply rooted, and older than the town hall clock (which, frankly, ran only when it felt like it). The townspeople called it “that grand old maple.” Anne called it “family.” Every spring it burst into leaves so thick and green that passing travelers slowed their carriages — and later their Fords — just to admire it. Children climbed it. Neighbors picnicked beneath it. One couple even got engaged under it, though that marriage did not last nearly as long as the tree. Then one April, a thin man from the town office arrived with a clipboard. “Good morning, Anne,” he said. “We’ve assessed your property.” “Well,” Anne replied, brushing soil from her hands, “that sounds thoughtful.” “It is,” he said gravely. “Your tree has been assigned a cess.” “A what?” “A cess. A local tax. It’s exceptionally large, historically significant, and provides shade to three households. The town feels it must contribute.” Anne stared at him. “You’re taxing my tree?” “Technically,” he adjusted his spectacles, “we are levying a horticultural enhancement cess.” Anne folded her arms and looked up at her great spreading branches. “Well,” she said slowly, “if you’re going to tax it, you’d better understand it.” And that’s when she did something remarkable. She began hanging small wooden plaques from the lower branches. On each she carved a name — her mother, her grandfather, a great-aunt who had once baked twelve pies in a single afternoon. She added dates, stories, small painted portraits. Soon the townspeople gathered beneath what was no longer merely a tree, but a living history. Children would point upward: “That branch is where Cousin Eliza went to sea.” “That knot in the trunk? That’s where Great-Grandfather lost his temper.” The thin man returned a month later with another clipboard. “I see,” he said carefully, “that the tree has… evolved.” “It’s not just a tree,” Anne replied. “It’s my Anne-cess tree.” “Your…?” “My Anne’s Cess Tree. You taxed it. So now it accounts for everyone who came before me.” The man blinked. Anne continued, warming to her theme. “You see, sir, before there was Ancestry, there was Anne’s Cess Tree — roots below, branches above, all connected. If you must assess it, you must assess us all.” By summer, the town quietly repealed the horticultural enhancement cess. No one wanted to be responsible for taxing history itself. And from that day on, whenever someone in Button Hollow asked about their family origins, Anne would smile and gesture toward the great maple. “Come stand beneath the Anne’s Cess Tree,” she’d say. “Every family has one. You just have to look up — and sometimes dig a little.”

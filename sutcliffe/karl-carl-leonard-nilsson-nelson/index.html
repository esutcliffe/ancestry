Carl Leonard Nelson: An Immigrant's Journey of Faith and Resilience

Carl Leonard Nelson was born Karl Leonard Nilsson on April 16, 1894, in Veinge (or Weinge), Halland, Sweden. His early years were spent on the family homestead in Mestocka, a rural setting that shaped his character and values. He was the son of Nils Peter Andersson and Kristina Pålsdotter. His original surname, Nilsson, followed the Scandinavian patronymic tradition, in which a child’s last name is derived from the father’s first name. Karl completed his education through the seventh grade. Standing at five feet, seven inches, he grew up likely immersed in the Lutheran faith, the predominant religion in Sweden at the time. The growing political unrest in Europe leading up to World War I may have influenced his decision to emigrate to the United States. Shortly after his 20th birthday, he embarked on a nine-day transatlantic journey aboard the RMS Alaunia, departing from Southampton, England, and arriving in Portland, Maine, on April 19, 1914. Carl’s early years in America were challenging. The onset of World War I only months after his arrival, followed by the devastating 1918–1919 influenza pandemic that claimed thousands of lives in Rhode Island, left deep impressions on him. Guided by a strong faith, he likely viewed these hardships as tests of spiritual endurance. Upon his arrival in the United States, Carl did not speak English. Resourceful and determined, he taught himself the language by studying the Sears Roebuck catalog, matching pictures with words and asking others to help him pronounce them. His dedication to self-improvement became a defining feature of his life. In his early years, Carl worked a series of manual labor jobs, including a difficult season as a cranberry bog worker in Massachusetts. The conditions were harsh: the men lived in a small shack with a shared bucket of water for both washing and drinking, and a second bucket for toileting. During the cold weather, the water often froze solid, rendering it unusable. Many workers suffered frostbite, with ears turning black from the cold. Carl endured the ordeal, later crediting both his physical strength and his faith in God for getting him through it. Determined to better himself, Carl also worked to reduce his Swedish accent, as Scandinavian workers were sometimes subjected to prejudice in factories and labeled “dumb Swedes.” Though he never completely lost his accent, he gained confidence through regular Bible reading and by listening to religious radio programs, both of which strengthened his English and his spiritual grounding. Around 1919, Carl secured employment at Samuel Moore & Company, a jewelry manufacturer located near 301 Friendship Street in Providence, Rhode Island. The building, now listed on the National Register of Historic Places, was an important fixture in the city’s industrial history. Carl worked there as a wire roller, a skilled trade he practiced faithfully for over four decades. In 1923, Carl returned to Sweden to escort his sister Signe to America. They sailed from Christiania (now Oslo, Norway) aboard the SS Frederik VIII, arriving in New York on December 1, 1923. At that time, Carl—by then using the anglicized surname Nelson—was 29 years old, while Signe was 21 and spoke only Swedish. Upon arrival, they stayed with Mrs. J. Sipples at 159 Pound Street, Providence. Carl’s deep spiritual convictions led him to join the Church of Jesus in 1926, where he attended services at a meeting hall located at 7 Winter Street, near his workplace. On September 20, 1927, he became a naturalized United States citizen, formally adopting the name Carl Leonard Nelson and continuing to reside at Pound Street. In 1928, at the age of 34, Carl married Evelyn Dodge, the 17-year-old daughter of the church’s minister. By 1930, the couple had moved in with Evelyn’s parents, Fred and Mabel Dodge, in accordance with Mrs. Dodge’s belief that God desired the families to live together. The 1930 United States Census records the Nelsons residing at 770 Westminster Street, Providence, in a tenement shared with other members of the church. The Great Depression (1929–1939) was a difficult period for many, including those within Carl’s faith community. In 1936, Carl made a $100 down payment on a small house at 222 Crescent Avenue in Cranston, believing he would own a share of the property. However, the title was placed solely in the names of Fred E. and Mabel C. Dodge. In 1937, another church member, Amos Bray, purchased three adjoining lots on Crescent Avenue for the construction of a larger house. In 1938, Carl invested $9,876.25—approximately $200,000 in 2025 currency—in the construction of the new house at 196 Crescent Avenue, yet again received no ownership rights. The 1940 Census shows sixteen individuals residing there, including the Dodge and Nelson families. On June 9, 1947, the church, led by Mrs. Dodge, evicted Carl and his family, effectively rendering them homeless. Undeterred, Carl pursued justice through the courts. In 1949, he won the landmark case Nelson v. Dodge, receiving $10,468.82 in damages. The case became a significant precedent in protecting parishioners from undue influence in religious financial matters. With the funds from the settlement, Carl purchased his permanent residence at 215 Garden Street, in the Eden Park neighborhood of Cranston, a community with a strong Swedish presence that was humorously nicknamed “Sweden Park.” Despite the profound personal betrayal he had suffered, Carl never lost his faith in God. Remarkably, he never spoke ill of those involved, nor did he display bitterness or resentment. Even when encountering those who had testified against him, he remained composed and gracious. His quiet strength and unwavering forgiveness became hallmarks of his character. Carl maintained a disciplined and healthy lifestyle. He preferred to ride his bicycle rather than drive, abstained from alcohol, and viewed the human body as a sacred gift from God. He often quoted Psalm 90:10: “The days of our years are threescore years and ten; and if by reason of strength they be fourscore years.” He loved nature, cultivated a large garden filled with fruit trees, and cherished the ocean shore, believing that salt water possessed healing properties. By the 1950 Census, Carl, aged 54, was recorded as a jeweler, while Evelyn, aged 39, worked as a delicatessen saleswoman. They lived with their five children in the family home on Garden Street. As their children grew and married, Carl and Evelyn moved to the upper floor of the house, renting the first floor to their daughter Ruth and her family. In time, Ruth purchased the house next door, and her daughter Miriam lived only a few doors away, creating a close-knit family neighborhood. Carl retired in 1961 after 42 years with Samuel Moore & Company. In November 1978, he and Evelyn celebrated their 50th wedding anniversary, surrounded by 180 relatives and friends. At that time, they were the proud parents of five children, sixteen grandchildren, and six great-grandchildren. During the celebration, Mayor-elect Edward DiPrete presented them with an official proclamation from Mayor Taft of Cranston, honoring their milestone. Throughout his life, Carl was known for his devotion to family, his modesty, his prudence, and his deep spiritual faith. His daily habits of prayer and reflection inspired those around him, and he was often called upon to lead prayers at family gatherings. In his later years, as his health declined, Carl became a resident of the Scandinavian Home in Cranston, where he received compassionate care. Carl Leonard Nelson passed away on April 5, 1986, at the age of 91. His life stands as a testament to perseverance, humility, and faith. From his humble beginnings in rural Sweden to his long and honorable life in America, Carl’s journey embodies the strength and resilience of the immigrant spirit — a legacy of courage and character that continues to inspire his descendants.

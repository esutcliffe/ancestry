Kansas City, Missouri May 23, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I promised to write you again today and I must keep my promise, but this is going to be a very short and poor letter. I have been shopping this morning and I am going again this afternoon. I am spending money right and left, and I think I will be a civilian tomorrow. I found most of my clothing ruined by moths, all but one suit, and it takes a litle fortune to buy clothes these days. But I think if my pocket book holds out, I will live through it. I am going to close this now and I will write you again soon. All my love to you dear. Always your Carl 3616 Main Street Kansas City, Missouri May 25, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I received your letter of the 23 yesterday, and I was so very glad to have it. It is 10 o’clock now, but I am going to write you a few lines before I go to bed. I went to church this morning and I have spent the day with the Pastor’s family, and I have had a fine time. I am going to blossom out in brand new civilian clothing in the morning and I expect to be at work Tuesday morning. I am going down to the Rock Flower Co. and help through Decoration Day rush, but I don’t think I will stay there. I am going to try to find outdoor work. Mr. Rock has been very good to me. He told whether I intended to work for him or not, to come down and get on the payroll until I could find something to suit me, and there is really nothing to do after Decoration Day. I am enclosing a copy of each of the pictures. They are not so very good, but I think some of them of you are just fine. I haven’t a thing to write about tonight, and I will close this, but I will write you again soon. I send you all my love, my dearest Hazel. Always your own, Carl Kansas City, Missouri May 27, 1919 My dearest Hazel, It is after 10 o’clock and I have just come home from work, and I found your letter of the 25th, and I was so very glad to have it. I went to work this morning with the Rock Flower Co. and I have put in a long day, and I am just a litle tired, for this is a very busy week for the florists, and I have promised to help them over Decoration Day. I don’t think I will stay with them so very long. It just seems to be impossible for me to work inside since I have been in the Army, and I am going to try and find work that will keep me outside. Yes, I am back in civilian clothing again, and I have had some time trying to tie a necktie and adjust my suspenders, and I felt very strange at first, but I am getting used to them now. And really, they are much more comfortable than the uniform. Yes, I expect you will be pretty busy this week. It is so much warmer now, and the cherries must be ripe. I wish I could be down there and help you. I am going to be so very busy the rest of this week. I will be working till midnight almost every night shipping out flowers. The flower business is better this year than it has ever been before, and I don’t think there will be flowers enough to supply the demand. But I must close and go to bed now. Good night, sweetheart. Always your own, Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 1, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I found your letter of the 28th waiting for me last night when I came home from work, and of course, I was so very glad to have it. It was a day late on account of Decoration Day, and I wanted to write you last night, but I was so awful tired. It has been a very busy week at the flower store. We worked till after 10 o’clock every night and Friday we didn’t get away from the store till midnight and started in again 4 o’clock Saturday a.m., and we were all pretty worn out. But the big rush is over now, although we will be pretty busy for another week or 10 days. There are always many parties and weddings the first 10 days in June. I am going to stay with Mr. Rock another week and possible two, but not any longer. I just can’t stand to work inside any more, and I am going to work outside just as soon as I can be spared in the flower store. I see some of the boys from my Company around town every day and they are all having the same trouble that I am. They can’t stand inside work anymore, and a good many are planning on going to the harvest fields the middle of the month. And Sweetheart, if I don’t find something to do between now and then, I am tempted to go myself. There is going to be some high wages paid in the wheat fields this summer. Tell me, dearest, will you care if I go out for about 6 weeks? There are thousands of men walking the streets now looking for work, and unless peace is signed before long, I think there will be many more. Business is not going to open up until they are sure that we are going to have peace. In about 10 days, there won’t be a thing to do in the floral business here, and I can’t see myself go idle in the city when they are crying for men in the wheat fields and paying from $5.00 to $7.00 a day. And you have had a letter from another boy in France, and he wants to come to see you. Well, dear, you want me to tell you what to do about it. You say you don’t want to see him. I believe if I were you I would write and tell him that you are engaged to another man, and that he can not come to see you. If he is a gentleman, he will not come and you won’t hear from him again. I sent you a box of flowers last Saturday, and I hope they reached you in good condition. The flowers are not as good this year on account of so much cold and wet weather, particular the roses. There hasn’t been any first class roses on the market this year. But I must tell you how I have spent the day. I got up 9 o’clock and had breakfast, and came back up to my room again with the intention of writing you a letter. I read your letter over again, and I got so awful lonesome for you dearest, and I wanted to be with you, but that was impossible and I decided then that I wanted to do the next best thing (be alone today), so I went out to Swope Park and I just roamed around out there all day. I came back for supper, and I am glad I did for it started to rain awful hard as soon as I reached the house, and it is still raining. It is 10 o’clock now and I am going to bed. I am sending you under separate cover the June number of the Journal. I have ordered it for you for another year beginning with July, and I am enclosing a watch for Donald. I send you all my love, dearest Hazel. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 3, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your letter of May 31 was here when I came home this evening. I knew I would have a letter from you today. I was looking for it yesterday. It had been sent to Neosho first by mistake, and it was a day late. I was so very glad for your letter and glad to know that the flowers reached you in good condition, but gladdest of all to know that they made you so happy. I wanted to send you some roses. I know you like them the best, but the roses are so poor this year on account of the wet weather, and they won’t stand to ship very well at this time of the year. We are not so busy at the store this week. I don’t get down till about 8 in the morning, and the last two days I have left there shortly after 5. The florist business comes in spells and I am looking for a spell the latter half of this week. And you wonder how I like the new life. Dearest Hazel, I am trying to be satisfied but I am not. The store seems so small and cramped, and I just can’t stand it, and I won’t stand it very much longer. I am going to get out where I will have more room, and I know now that I will never live in a large city. You said in one of your letters that you wished I could find work near Seneca. Well, Sweetheart, I am going to be down in your part of the country before the summer is over. I love the country down there as I love you, and I know I will be happy down there with you. I feel pretty good while I am working, but when evening comes and I come out to my room, I get so dredfully lonesome for you, and I wish I was down there. And you are going to send me a box of cherries. That is awful nice of you dear. And yes, I will be looking for them, and I am preparing for a real feast. I am very fond of them when they are good and ripe. I will close now. It is after 9 and past my bedtime. I send you all my love, my own dearest Hazel. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 6, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your letter of the 4th came today and I was so glad to have it. I received the box of cherries two days ago. They came through in fine condition, and I was so glad for them. They were so delicious and fine, and the fact that you picked them all made them taste so much better. I came home from work early this evening. We did not have much to do, and I left at 4 o’clock and I went out in the outskirts of the city and had supper with some friends of mine, and I have just come back. It is almost 11 o’clock, but I must write you a few lines before I go to bed, for I want you to have a letter Sunday morning. We expect to have a rather busy day tomorrow and also Sunday, but I don’t work Sundays. I haven’t had any Sundays for nearly 3 years and I am going to try to have a litle time off now. I think I deserve it, don’t you dear? And Donald was so tickled with the watch. Well, I am awful glad of it. I wish I could have seen him when he unwrapped it. He is a mighty fine boy and I like him very much, and he has the sweetest and dearest sister in the world, and I am thinking and dreaming of her all the time. But I am wondering who the person is who paid me such compliments, but I am not going to ask you to tell me if you don’t want to. Sweetheart, this is such a poor letter, but I will try to do better next time. I want to thank you for the cherries. They were so fine, and I enjoyed them so much. With all my love to you dear. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 9, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your dear letter came today, and it was such a fine letter, and I am going to write you again tonight. We have had real summer here the last 3 days. It was very hot yesterday. We had an electric storm this morning between 8 and 9. It got almost dark. We had to turn on the lights in the store. It cleared off again before noon, and it is real warm again. And I want to get outside more than ever now. I think this will be my last week in the store. Mr. Rock is in New York. If he gets home this week then I expect to leave him. I haven’t decided what to do yet, but it is very likely that I will go to the harvest fields for 6 weeks or 2 months, and Sweetheart, I think when I leave Kansas City again, I will leave it for good. I don’t feel home here any more, and the hills are calling me. I learned to love them so in the short time I was there, and I know I will be happier down there with you than any other place. Dearest Hazel, are you glad to have me tell you that next time I come down there, I am coming down there to stay? I went out to Independence yesterday and spent the day with a florist friend of mine. In the evening, we came in to K.C. in a motor car and went to a show, and I had a very pleasant day, but all day long my thoughts were with you, dear. I am going to Lawrence tomorrow morning and decorate a church and a home for a wedding, and I expect to be back here again by evening. I am going to close this now dear. It is 9 o’clock and I want to read the peace treaty before I go to bed. Good night dearest. But I am more convinced than ever now that I can never live in a large city again. It seems so awful crowded and stuffy, and I think I will go to the harvest fields next week, and I know it will do me a lot of good. But I must close now. It is way past my bed time. Good night, my dearest Hazel. I have fixed a lily pond in Mr. Rock’s window and it is attracting a great deal of attention. There are about 250 lilies in bloom now. Sweetheart, I know you will excuse this short letter this time, won’t you? I send you all my love. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 17, 1919 My dearest Hazel, I had 2 fine letters from you yesterday. One was dated June 14th and the other was dated April 8th, and it had been all over France. But I was glad to have it even if it was over 2 months old. Well, Sweetheart, it seems that our plans are changed sometimes by some higher power, and my plans have been changed. I was crossing the street down town last Sunday and I slipped and hit my knee on the street car rail and injured my knee cap to the extent that I won’t be able to go to the harvest fields. Now don’t be scared dear. It is not a bit dangerous. Just a litle painful. I stayed in bed yesterday but I am getting around today. Dr. Gist is taking care of me and he said I had better not try to go out and do any heavy labor for about 60 days, and so I have had to change my plans entirely. And I bought me a new Ford car this morning. I think it will be delivered to me tomorrow, and I am going to run a jitney, at least until my knee gets well enough so I can do something else. The street car service is very poor up here, and there are several hundred cars running here every day, and they are doing fine. In fact, I have a friend running a car and he paid for it in 60 days doing jitney work. I am going to send you “Their Yesterdays” in a day or two. I loaned it out to one of my neighbors a few days ago. And I am also going to send you “The Call of the Wild” by Jack London. I think it is a splendid book. But Sweetheart, it is so hot here. I am about to smother. I am going to close this now and go over to Hyde Park. It is only a block from here and I can get around that far. All my love is yours, dearest. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 19, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your dear letter of June 16 came yesterday, and I was so glad to have it. And I had really intended to answer you last night, and the only excuse I can give you for not writing is that I have a new motor car. My knee is getting along pretty good. Of course, I can’t walk very good, but I can operate an automobile and I have been out in it all day. And I am sorry to tell you that I have had my first accident. I ran into a lamp post this evening and wrecked my car, and it is in the shop now, and it will cost me about $40.00 to have it repaired again. Now Sweetheart, I expect you to think I am getting very reckless and very bad up here, but I am not. I drove nearly every make of car in France but a Ford, and I thought I knew how to drive one of them, but I discovered that I didn’t. I wrecked it driving in to the garage, but I knew I will not make the same mistake twice. I am might glad to know that the flowers reached you in good condition, but I don’t see how they could be in good condition after they had been on the road for 48 hours. I am awful sorry to know they were delayed so much. I mailed them to you Friday noon so you would be sure to get them Saturday. It is so dreadful hot up here these days. People are dying from heat every day. I wonder if it is so hot down where you are. I will get my car out of the shop in the morning and I am going to be much more careful in the future. I am going to run it around town a day or two before I carry any passengers. I am going to close this now and go to bed. Good night dearest. But somehow, your last letter tells me that you are just a litle troubled and worried about me, and particular about my new work. Then I had the injury, and I thought the thing over, and I thought of you dearest. I have asked you to be my wife, and you have promised me you would. Hazel, I intend to make a home for you in the near future, and I intend to make you as comfortable and as happy as I can. And I felt that I could not afford to be idle from 30 to 60 days at this time of the year, and I decided it was the best thing I could do. I hope, darling, that you will look at this the way I do. I felt it was the only thing I could do, and I want to tell you that I was thinking of you more than of myself when I made the decision. And you are afraid that I will get used to city life again and will forget about you and your hills. Never, dear. I have promised you that I will come, and I am not going to disappoint you. I don’t like city life any more and I never will. I still love your hills as dearly as I love you, and I can never forget you nor them. And I am going to tell you again that I will be down there to stay before the summer is over. Please, Hazel, don’t never think that your letters are beginning to losing (?) to me. They are dearer to me than anything else (except yourself). I know my letters have been very poor lately, and I know I didn’t answer your questions. I will tell you, I was pretty disappointed and pretty blue over my accident, and for a few days it was pretty painful, but I was most disappointed because my plans were all upset, and now I am going to answer your question. You asked me if you were better off down in your hills than if you had been out in the world, and yes, I am sure you are. It has been my experience that traveling around in this world breeds discontent. I am sure I should be much more content had I not seen as much of the world as I have. And you felt that you would not be able to walk hand in hand with me because I have been around and seen so much. Why dear, you have a much better education than I have and you have such perfect understanding of things, and you are so broadminded and cultured, and I wish I could make you understand how happyt I am that I ever came to know you and love you. And I am just so awful proud of you dear. And you wonder how I ever came to love you. Well, I can’t explain that to you dear, only there was something so big and so fine about you, and I just had to love you. But my letter is getting rather long and I must be closing or you will get tired of reading it. I am glad to tell you that my knee is getting better every day. I can’t walk very much yet, but I can drive a car. I am going to start in driving in the morning. I am going to send you a book in a day or two. Would you like to have the Kodak again, dear? If you want it, let me know and I will send it to you. You will not forget that I love you as always, and that I am coming down in your hills to live with you. I send you all my love. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 24, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your letter came yesterday and you said it was a poor letter. Well, I didn’t think so. I was just the gladdest ever for it. Always your own Carl Kansas City, Missouri June 29, 1919 My dearest Hazel, Your good letter of the 27th came yesterday, and I wanted to answer you last night, but I was so awful tired.

Charles Frederick Bock

Born in the Empire, Raised in America

Let me tell you about Charles Frederick Bock, a boy from northern Germany who grew up in a New Jersey farm town and spent the better part of a century working with his hands. Carly, he is your great-great-grandfather: his son Charles F. Bock Jr. married Alice Frances Duhamel, and their son, Donald Paul Bock, is your grandfather. Charles was born on December 19, 1877, in Holstein, a northern province of the newly unified German Empire, to Henry Christian Bock and Augusta C. Bock. He had blue eyes and brown hair, and he arrived at a time of enormous change in his homeland, as industry, shifting economies and new political structures were remaking German life. In 1881, when Charles was only three, his parents made the big decision to leave for the United States with their four children. Like many families from Holstein and the rest of northern Germany, the Bocks were looking for the better prospects and steadier life that America’s growing industrial cities seemed to offer. They settled at last in New Jersey, where a strong German-American community kept the old customs alive while learning the rhythms of a new country. Charles grew up in Springfield, in Union County, a township that around the turn of the century was still country at heart. Its open fields and small mills were a world away from the factory bustle of nearby Newark and Elizabeth, where many of the neighbors went to work. In 1900, at twenty-two, Charles was a farm hand, working the land and building the steady habits of hard work and reliability that would mark his whole life. On October 19, 1910, he married Eva Mae Anderson in Newark, and the two of them made their home in Springfield, where they stayed for the rest of their lives. They raised seven children: Charles Jr., Edward, Marian, Robert, Lillian, Franklin and Richard. Charles’s father, Henry Christian Bock, lived close by until he died in 1923, which kept the whole extended family rooted in the same close-knit town. By the First World War, Charles had moved from farm work into the mechanical trades. From 1918 to 1920 he worked as a pipefitter at Duesenberg Motors in Elizabeth, one of the most respected car makers in the country. Duesenberg built luxury cars and racing engines and needed highly skilled men. Charles kept up the factory’s intricate system of steam and fuel lines, work that called for precision and responsibility. In the 1930s and 1940s, the census lists him as a yard boss for a lumber and building supply company, overseeing the crews, the shipments and the materials. That was a step up, and it says a good deal about how much he was trusted. In his sixties, during the 1940s, he went to work for the Nicholas Schmidt Company in Springfield, a local metal-working and fabrication firm that served builders and manufacturers during and after World War II. That he kept working so far into later life tells you something about his stamina, his skill and his quiet professionalism. A German-born Man in Two Wars We don’t know whether Charles faced any of this, but it’s worth knowing what German Americans of his generation lived through. During the First World War, suspicion of anything German ran high across the country. Theodore Roosevelt warned in 1915 that there was “not room in this country for hyphenated Americanism.” Once the United States entered the war in 1917, German-language newspapers were shut down or driven out of business, schools dropped German from their courses, and German-speaking churches were pushed to give up their German services. German-sounding names of foods, towns and streets were changed, and German American men, however long they had lived here, hurried to prove their loyalty. Beginning in late 1917, German-born residents aged fourteen and older who had not become citizens had to register with the government as “enemy aliens,” carry a registration card, and get permission to travel or move. More than 480,000 registered, and several thousand were arrested. During the Second World War, German nationals once again had to register, and some were interned. Charles was German-born, and those wartime years fell right when he was working at Duesenberg and later in Springfield’s yards and shops. The records hint at where he stood. In the 1900 census, the column for his naturalization is blank. When he filled out his draft card in September 1918, at age forty and working as a pipe fitter at Duesenberg, he was marked a “Declarant”: he had filed his first papers toward citizenship but was not yet a citizen, and his country was listed as Germany. That would mean that during the war he was one of the German-born noncitizens the enemy-alien rules were written for, and those rules would have applied to him. We found no record that he registered as an enemy alien, and we don’t know how any of it touched him or his family. His father, Henry, appears as naturalized in the censuses, but the records suggest Charles did not gain citizenship through his father; census records indicate he was naturalized in his own right about 1919. What we do know is that he stayed put, kept working, and raised his children in the same town through both wars. Later Years Charles and Eva lived long enough to watch Springfield turn from a quiet farming town into a modern suburb. Eva died in 1961, and Charles lived another twelve years. He died in 1973 at the age of ninety-six. His life bridged two worlds, the farm villages of Holstein and the industrial towns of New Jersey, and it showed the steady resilience of a man who built his life on honest work and devotion to his family. Though he was born in Germany, he was raised entirely in America, the first of his family to put down real roots in the United States. Through a lifetime of labor, craftsmanship and quiet dignity, Charles Frederick Bock helped shape his community and the generations that followed him.
Conflicting records

The records disagree on when the Bocks came to America: the 1900 and 1920 censuses say 1881, and the 1930 census says 1879. We’ve used 1881 here. They also disagree on when Henry Christian Bock became a citizen: the 1900 census already shows him as naturalized, while the 1920 census gives 1904. We haven’t settled on a date.

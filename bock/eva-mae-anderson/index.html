Eva Mae (Anderson) Bock (1890–1961)

The Heart of a Large Springfield Family

Carly, this is the story of your great-great-grandmother, Eva Mae Anderson. Her son, Charles F. Bock Jr., married Alice Frances Duhamel, and their son, Donald Paul Bock, is your grandfather.Eva was born in New Jersey in July 1890, though one census says 1891. Her parents were David M. Anderson and Phoebe E. Anderson, who also appears in the records as Etta, and we believe Phoebe’s maiden name was Compton. Eva grew up in Springfield, in Union County, when the township was still marked by open meadows, dairy farms, and the sound of nearby mills. She had an older brother, Harry, and younger brothers whose names are not recorded the same way from one census to the next. We imagine a little girl walking to the schoolhouse and helping at home.By her later teens the family was living on Westfield Avenue in Springfield, and Eva found work close to home as an edger at Butter Bass Manufacturing, one of the small creameries that dotted Union County in the early 1900s. These plants churned and packaged butter for the growing markets in Newark and New York, and young women like Eva often handled the cutting, smoothing, and wrapping of the finished blocks. It was steady, practical work that added to the household income while keeping her near family, and we imagine her coming back along Westfield Avenue at the end of a long day, with the smell of the creamery still on her clothes.On Wednesday evening, October 19, 1910, Eva married Charles Frederick Bock at her parents’ home on Westfield Avenue. She was about twenty, and Charles, the son of Henry and Augusta Bock of Tooker Avenue, was thirty-two. The Rev. Fred B. Curry, pastor of the Methodist Church, officiated. Eva’s brother Harry stood as best man, and Charles’s sister Amanda was bridesmaid. Eva wore a dress of Alice blue pongee, and Amanda wore tan pongee. After the ceremony there was a wedding supper, with family and friends from Westfield, East Orange, and Springfield. Among the guests were her grandparents, Mr. and Mrs. Henry Compton. The newspapers said the young couple would make their home in Newark.By the time their first child was born the next year, they were in Springfield, and the children came one after another: Charles Jr. in 1911, Edward Clifford in 1913, Marian in 1918, Ruth around 1920, Robert in 1922, Lillian in 1925, Franklin in 1926 or 1927, and Richard in 1930. Eva and Charles raised eight children.By 1930 the family was living on Mountain Avenue in Springfield, a busy house in the lean years of the Depression. Charles worked in the trades and later as a yard boss in the lumber yards, and Eva ran the household. We imagine the table on Mountain Avenue growing longer as the children arrived, and Eva stretching a working wage as far as it would go.They spent most of their lives in Springfield, though around 1949 they were living in nearby Millburn. It was in those years that they lost their son Edward, who died on February 25, 1949, at only thirty-five. His widow, Constance, came to live with Charles and Eva afterward. It must have been a heavy time in that house.In the years that followed they returned to Springfield, for a time on Tooker Avenue, the street where Charles had grown up. Their grown sons Franklin, by then a Springfield firefighter, and Richard, then in the Navy, were under the same roof again for a while. Later Eva and Charles lived with their son Robert. Eva died in Springfield on August 7, 1961, at seventy-one. Charles lived another twelve years. They are buried together at Presbyterian Cemetery in Springfield, under a double stone inscribed “CHARLES F. 1877—1973 / EVA M. 1890—1961.”
Charles and Eva Bock’s gravestone, Presbyterian Cemetery, Springfield, New Jersey. Photo by NotYourTime, Find a Grave memorial 137461906.
She had spent nearly her whole life in and around that one New Jersey town, raising a large family through war and Depression, and she belongs, with them, on the Bock family tree.

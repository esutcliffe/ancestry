Peter Nicholas Madamas (1890–1949)

A Trusting Cobbler with a Disability

Brynn and Fallon, this is the story of your great-great-grandfather, Peter Nicholas Madamas, the father of your great-grandmother Aphrodite “Affy” Madamas and the husband of Mary Strattos.Peter was born on June 15, 1890, in Metaline, Greece. That is the birthplace he gave on his 1942 draft card, and Metaline is an old European spelling of Mytilene (Mételin in French), the port city and capital of the island of Lesbos. When Peter was a boy, Lesbos was still part of the Ottoman Empire. We imagine him knowing the bustle of the harbor, with its boats and its quay lined with shops and offices. When a young man left for America in those years, we suspect no one saw it as abandoning home. It was a sensible way to help the family and build a future of his own.

Peter was only fifteen when he arrived in New York on April 22, 1906. We can hardly picture crossing an ocean alone at that age. He had to learn a new language and a new way of life and find work, all on his own.By the mid-1910s Peter was starting a family in New England. His firstborn, a son named John, was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, around 1914 and died within days. Charles Peter Madamas followed on March 26, 1915, in Springfield, and Bessie was born in Connecticut around 1917.Peter found his trade in shoes. In July 1918 a newspaper described him as a shoemaker with his own shop on Main Street in Thompsonville, Connecticut. He had just hired a new helper and stepped out to tend to some business in Springfield. When he came back, the helper was gone, the money from the cash register was gone with him, and the shop had been left unlocked. We read that story as a picture of a small-business owner who trusted people, maybe more than he should have. Shoemaking was a good trade for an immigrant. It cost little to start, it depended on skilled hands rather than big machines, and people always needed their shoes fixed.The 1920 census found Peter, Mary, Charles and Bessie in Enfield, Connecticut. That year Peter was listed as a laborer in a carpet mill, one of the region’s toughest factory jobs. Set beside the 1918 shop, we suspect he was working for wages while keeping up his shoemaking on the side, as many immigrants did. He could read and write, but he was still learning English and was not yet a citizen.Through the 1920s the hard work paid off. By 1930 the family had settled in Springfield, renting a home on Bradford Street, with six children and a boarder in the house. The census now listed Peter as the owner of his own shoe-repair business. He spoke English by then and had begun the steps toward becoming a citizen.Somewhere in these cobbler years, we believe, Peter began to lose his hearing. He had learned English by ear as a young man, so the loss must have come later. In time he may have become completely deaf. His speech stayed fine, though, and he became a good lip reader, and we think that makes everything he built all the more remarkable.Affy was born in June 1931, the youngest, joining Charles, Bessie, Helen, Nicholas, Irene and Anna. Peter was old-fashioned and protective of his daughters. He didn’t allow lipstick, not even when the girls were in their late teens and twenties! The family story is that Mary would slip them some, so they could put it on once they were out of the house and wipe it off before they came home. We like to imagine the giggling on the front steps.Springfield stayed home through the Great Depression. Peter turns up in city records again in 1935, still keeping a roof over a big family at a time when many families struggled just to stay housed. By 1940 they were renting on Dwight Street, and Peter, at forty-nine, was repairing shoes full time in a department store. We suspect he traded the risks of his own shop for steadier pay in hard years. He earned $975 in 1939, not much for a family that size, but reliable. The census gave his schooling as eighth grade, and he was signed up for Social Security, the new government retirement program.Peter kept working almost to the end. He spent his last years with the Poole Dry Goods Company, a department store on Main Street in Springfield, and the city directories for 1948 and 1949 list him there as a department manager, living with Mary on Carver Street. He died in Springfield on September 29, 1949, at fifty-nine, when Affy was just eighteen. By the 1952 directory Mary was listed as “widow of Peter.”

From the island of Lesbos to his own shoe shop, through factory work and the Depression, Peter spent his life quietly providing for a growing family. We think of him as a man of skill and patience who trusted people and worked hard, and who never let his hearing hold him back.A note on his birthplacePeter’s obituary in the Springfield Republican on September 29, 1949, gives a different birthplace: Agiasos (printed “Agiassou”), a mountain farming village on Lesbos near Mary’s home village of Ambeliko. We have kept Metaline, the answer Peter himself gave on his draft card.
